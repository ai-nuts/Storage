## Problem
**Necessary:** Momentum SGD (mSGD) and AdaGrad are empirically superior to SGD, yet their theoretical convergence for smooth, possibly non-convex loss functions remains poorly established.
**Additional:** Most prior results only guarantee subsequence convergence or convergence of time averages, which is weaker than the almost-sure asymptotic convergence practitioners rely on.
**Audio script:** Stochastic gradient descent and its accelerated variants are the workhorses of modern machine learning. Two of the most important variants are momentum-based SGD, which averages past gradients to smooth the update direction, and AdaGrad, which adapts its step size using the accumulated gradient magnitude. Both consistently outperform vanilla SGD in practice. Despite this, their theoretical foundations are surprisingly incomplete. For general smooth loss functions, which may be non-convex as in deep networks, existing analyses only manage to prove weak forms of convergence, such as the convergence of a subsequence of iterates or the convergence of time averages. These fall short of the stronger, almost-sure asymptotic convergence that would actually justify their use.

## Motivation
**Necessary:** A rigorous convergence theory for static-momentum mSGD and standard AdaGrad, without convexity, bounded-iterate, or uniformly bounded-gradient-noise assumptions, is still missing.
**Additional:** Prior work handles only time-varying momentum or modified AdaGrad variants; the widely used static momentum coefficient and the norm-form AdaGrad in practice fall outside those conditions.
**Audio script:** Why revisit these algorithms now? Because the gap between what practitioners use and what theory covers is wide. Existing convergence proofs for momentum methods typically require a time-varying momentum coefficient that shrinks to zero, whereas practitioners fix the momentum at a static value like zero point nine. Similarly, the AdaGrad results in the literature often apply to a modified version of the algorithm, not the standard norm-based form actually deployed. On top of that, many prior analyses lean on strong assumptions: convex loss functions, bounded iterates, or gradient noise that is uniformly bounded over the entire parameter space. This paper aims to relax all of these and analyze the algorithms as they are truly used.

## Contribution
**Necessary:** The paper proves almost-sure asymptotic convergence of mSGD to a connected set of stationary points, quantifies its convergence rate to explain the acceleration over SGD, and proves almost-sure asymptotic convergence of standard norm-form AdaGrad.
**Additional:** Results hold for possibly non-convex loss functions under relaxed noise assumptions, extending subsequence and time-average convergence results in the literature to the stronger asymptotic sense.
**Audio script:** The paper makes three main contributions. First, it proves that the iterates of momentum SGD are asymptotically convergent, with probability one, to a connected set of stationary points, even for non-convex losses. This is stronger and more general than the subsequence convergence results that came before. Second, it quantifies the convergence rate of mSGD, giving a theoretical explanation for why momentum can be viewed as an acceleration of SGD, and it connects this rate to the convergence of time averages. Third, it proves that the iterates of standard AdaGrad also converge almost surely to a connected set of stationary points, again for non-convex losses, extending the weaker subsequence-convergence results in the literature.

## Method
**Necessary:** Using stochastic approximation techniques, the authors treat mSGD and AdaGrad as noisy dynamical systems and show their iterates track a limiting ODE toward the stationary set. A stability lemma bounds the expected loss, and a relaxed noise condition controls gradient fluctuations without requiring a uniform bound over the whole space.
**Additional:** For mSGD, a decreasing step size satisfying the Robbins-Monro conditions counteracts noise while the momentum coefficient stays static; for AdaGrad, the adaptive (random, gradient-dependent) step size is handled by carefully bounding the conditionally dependent terms that block naive conditional-expectation arguments.
**Key equation:** `$v_n = \alpha v_{n-1} + \varepsilon_n \nabla_{\theta_n} g(\theta_n,\xi_n),\quad \theta_{n+1}=\theta_n - v_n$` (mSGD); `$S_n = S_{n-1} + \|\nabla_{\theta_n} g(\theta_n,\xi_n)\|^2,\quad \theta_{n+1}=\theta_n - \tfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{S_n}}\nabla_{\theta_n} g(\theta_n,\xi_n)$` (AdaGrad, norm form); `$\mathbb{E}\|\nabla_{\theta_n} g(\theta_n)\|^2 = O\!\left(e^{-\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{s\varepsilon_i}{p(1-\alpha)^2}}\right)$` (mSGD rate)
**Audio script:** The analysis rests on stochastic approximation theory, which views these iterative algorithms as discrete-time noisy approximations of an underlying continuous dynamical system. The core idea is to show the iterates asymptotically follow the gradient flow toward the set of stationary points. A key stability lemma guarantees that the expected loss stays bounded throughout training. Crucially, the authors replace the usual assumption that gradient noise is uniformly bounded over all of parameter space, which fails for quadratic or cubic losses, with a milder condition that bounds the noise relative to the loss value itself. For momentum SGD, a decreasing step size satisfying the classical Robbins-Monro summability conditions tames the randomness while the momentum coefficient is held static. AdaGrad is harder, because its adaptive step size is a random quantity that depends on the current gradient noise, breaking the usual conditional-expectation manipulations; the authors develop bounds that overcome exactly this dependency.

## Dataset / Benchmark
**Necessary:** This is a purely theoretical paper: there are no datasets, benchmarks, or empirical experiments. All results are mathematical theorems proven under stated assumptions.
**Additional:** The loss functions considered are general smooth (continuously differentiable), non-negative, possibly non-convex functions whose gradients are Lipschitz; example families satisfying the assumptions include periodic and polynomial functions such as sin²(x) and (x−1)(x−2)(x−3)(x−4).
**Audio script:** It is worth stressing that this work is entirely theoretical. There are no datasets, no benchmark tasks, and no numerical experiments. Instead, the contribution is a set of rigorously proven convergence theorems. The objects of study are general loss functions that are non-negative, continuously differentiable, and have Lipschitz-continuous gradients, but which may be non-convex and may have multiple local critical points, as is typical in deep learning. The authors illustrate the breadth of their assumptions with concrete non-convex examples such as sine-squared and quartic polynomial functions.

## Key Result
**Necessary:** Under mild assumptions, mSGD iterates converge almost surely to a connected set of stationary points (Theorem 1), and standard norm-form AdaGrad iterates do the same (Theorem 3), both for non-convex losses.
**Additional:** These almost-sure asymptotic results are strictly stronger than the subsequence-convergence and time-average results they extend from prior literature.
**Audio script:** The headline results are two almost-sure asymptotic convergence theorems. The first establishes that the iterates of momentum SGD converge, with probability one, to a connected set of stationary points of the loss; when that set reduces to a single point, the iterates converge to that stationary point. The second theorem establishes the same almost-sure asymptotic convergence for standard norm-based AdaGrad. Both hold for possibly non-convex loss functions and under relaxed noise assumptions. Together they lift the weaker subsequence and time-average guarantees found in earlier work up to the much stronger notion of asymptotic convergence for every realization.

## Ablation Study
**Necessary:** As a theory paper there are no experimental ablations; instead the analysis isolates the effect of the momentum coefficient α by deriving the convergence rate as a function of α.
**Additional:** Setting α = 0 recovers the SGD rate exactly, and increasing α toward one reduces the rate coefficient, showing momentum's acceleration analytically rather than empirically.
**Audio script:** In place of an empirical ablation, the paper offers an analytic one: it studies how the convergence-rate bound depends on the momentum coefficient. When the momentum coefficient is set to zero, the derived rate collapses exactly to the known convergence rate of plain SGD. As the momentum coefficient increases toward one, the exponent in the rate grows and the associated coefficient shrinks, meaning faster convergence. Pushing the momentum close to one drives the time-average convergence rate toward order one over T. This dependence isolates the role of momentum precisely, playing the role that an ablation study would in an empirical paper.

## Headline Numbers
**Necessary:** mSGD mean-square-gradient rate is $O(e^{-\sum_{i=1}^n s\varepsilon_i / (p(1-\alpha)^2)})$; with α = 0 this equals the SGD rate, and as α→1 the time-average rate reaches order $O(1/T)$.
**Additional:** Results require only Robbins-Monro step sizes (∑εₙ = ∞, ∑εₙ² < ∞, e.g. εₙ = 1/n) and a relaxed noise bound E‖∇g(θ) − ∇g(θ,ξ)‖² ≤ M(1 + g(θ)), with no convexity or bounded-iterate assumptions.
**Audio script:** Because this is a theory paper, the key numbers are convergence rates rather than accuracies. For momentum SGD, the expected squared gradient norm decays at a rate on the order of an exponential whose exponent scales with the sum of step sizes divided by the quantity p times one minus alpha squared. Setting the momentum to zero recovers precisely the SGD rate, confirming consistency. As the momentum approaches one, the corresponding time-average convergence rate improves to order one over T. All of this holds under only the classical Robbins-Monro step-size conditions, for example a step size of one over n, together with a relaxed noise condition that bounds the gradient variance by a constant times one plus the loss, and without any convexity or bounded-iterate requirement.

## Takeaway
**Necessary:** With probability one, both static-momentum mSGD and standard AdaGrad converge to stationary points of general non-convex losses, and momentum provably accelerates SGD.
**Additional:** The proofs relax convexity, bounded-iterate, and uniform noise assumptions, closing much of the gap between the theory and the practice of these ubiquitous optimizers.
**Audio script:** The lasting message is a reassuring one for practitioners. Under mild and realistic conditions, both momentum SGD with a fixed momentum coefficient and standard AdaGrad are guaranteed to converge, almost surely, to stationary points of general, possibly non-convex loss functions. Moreover, the analysis shows analytically that momentum genuinely accelerates SGD. By relaxing the convexity, bounded-iterate, and uniform-noise assumptions common in earlier proofs, this work substantially narrows the long-standing gap between the strong empirical performance of these optimizers and their theoretical understanding.

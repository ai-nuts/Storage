# Problem

Core claim: Official estimates of the global ocean carbon sink from the annual Global Carbon Budget lag actual conditions by roughly one year, so recent changes in ocean CO2 uptake cannot be monitored in time.

Supporting detail: The delay stems from the computational cost of ocean biogeochemical models and from the roughly one-year lag of the underlying SOCAT surface-ocean CO2 observations that data products depend on.

Narration: The ocean is one of the largest buffers against climate change, soaking up a big fraction of the carbon dioxide we emit. But our best official accounting of how much it absorbs, the annual Global Carbon Budget, always trails reality by about a year. That means when the ocean's uptake shifts, we simply cannot see it until long after the fact. This lag is baked in: the biogeochemical models are computationally heavy, and the surface-ocean observations they rely on are themselves delayed by roughly a year.

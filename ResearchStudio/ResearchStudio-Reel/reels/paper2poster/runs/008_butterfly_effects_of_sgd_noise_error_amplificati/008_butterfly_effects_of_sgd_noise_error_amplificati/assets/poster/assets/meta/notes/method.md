# Method

Core claim: The authors empirically disentangle statistical from computational (algorithmic) causes of reward oscillation, isolating GVA — the amplification of minibatch SGD gradient variance through unstable closed-loop rollouts. They then test variance-reduction remedies (LR decay, large batches via gradient accumulation, and iterate averaging), finding EMA of the optimization trajectory stabilizes rollouts with essentially no downside, and back this with a convex-optimization theory vignette on a discontinuous "cliff loss".

Supporting detail: EMA is recommended with an initial burn-in, a polynomial-decay schedule for the averaging rate, and updates at every iterate; in language modeling EMA further removes the need for full learning-rate decay, reducing overfitting.

Narration: The approach is first to carefully separate the two possible causes of the oscillations. Is it a statistical problem, meaning not enough data, or a computational one, meaning something about the optimization itself? By ablating away the statistical difficulty and still seeing the oscillations, the authors pin the blame on gradient variance amplification. Then they test the natural fixes. Aggressively decaying the learning rate helps, and dramatically increasing the batch size helps, but both cost a lot of compute. The star of the paper is much cheaper. You simply keep a running exponential moving average of your weights, updating it every step, defined by theta-tilde at time t plus one equals one minus gamma times the old average plus gamma times the new iterate. A supporting theorem shows that in a simple deterministic environment, a tiny perturbation that barely changes the imitation loss can blow up the rollout reward gap exponentially in the horizon.

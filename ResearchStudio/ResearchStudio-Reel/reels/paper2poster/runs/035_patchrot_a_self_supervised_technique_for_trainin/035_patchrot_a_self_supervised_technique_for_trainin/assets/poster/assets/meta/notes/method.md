# Method

Core claim: Given an image, PatchRot either rotates the whole image or rotates each patch by a random multiple of 90° (0/90/180/270) and trains the ViT to predict every rotation angle as a 4-way classification. The class token's MLP head predicts the image rotation (global); new added MLP heads on each patch token predict that patch's rotation (local). After pretraining, the extra heads are discarded and only the class-token head is fine-tuned on the downstream task.

Supporting detail: To stop the model from solving patch rotation via edge continuity, patches are cut from a larger grid of size P+B and randomly cropped, leaving a random 0-to-2B pixel buffer gap. Self-supervised training runs at a reduced resolution and downstream fine-tuning uses the original resolution with interpolated positional embeddings. Image and patch rotations are applied in separate forward passes (not simultaneously), and the two passes share encoder weights.

Narration: PatchRot works as follows. Take an input image and either rotate the whole image, or rotate each of its patches independently, by a random multiple of ninety degrees: zero, ninety, one-eighty, or two-seventy. The vision transformer is then trained to predict these rotation angles as a simple four-way classification. The transformer's class token, which normally predicts the object category, is repurposed to predict the whole-image rotation, capturing global structure. New multilayer-perceptron heads are attached to each patch token to predict that individual patch's rotation, capturing local detail. To prevent the network from cheating, patches are carved from a slightly larger grid and randomly cropped, so a random buffer gap sits between them and edge continuity can't give away the answer. Training happens at a reduced resolution; then the extra patch heads are removed and the network is fine-tuned at full resolution on the real classification task, with positional embeddings interpolated to the larger patch count.

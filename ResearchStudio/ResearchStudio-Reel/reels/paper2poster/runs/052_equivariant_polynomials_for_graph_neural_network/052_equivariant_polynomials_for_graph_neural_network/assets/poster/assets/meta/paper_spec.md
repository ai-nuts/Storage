## Problem
**Necessary:** Graph neural networks have inherently limited expressive power, and the Weisfeiler-Lehman (WL) hierarchy used to measure it has a complex, hard-to-act-on definition and is too coarse to separate current GNNs.
**Additional:** WL gives no direct guidance for improving a model, so practitioners lack a constructive target for building more powerful architectures.
**Audio script:** The expressive power of graph neural networks, meaning which functions of a graph they can actually represent, is known to be bounded. The field measures this power with the Weisfeiler-Lehman hierarchy, a family of graph-isomorphism tests. But WL has two problems. Its definition is combinatorial and gives no recipe for making a network stronger, and its rungs are so far apart that many architecturally different GNNs collapse to the same WL level. The paper asks for a finer, more actionable measure of expressive power.

## Motivation
**Necessary:** A good expressiveness measure should be interpretable and constructive, pointing directly at operations a model is missing, rather than only certifying pass or fail on an isomorphism test.
**Additional:** Prior work characterized only low-order equivariant maps or leaned on WL, leaving a gap for a complete, computation-friendly description of what a GNN should be able to output.
**Audio script:** The authors argue that the right way to grade a graph network is by the concrete quantities it can compute. Polynomials of the adjacency matrix that respect node relabeling, called equivariant graph polynomials, are natural candidates: subgraph counts and many structural features are exactly such polynomials. If we knew the full space of these polynomials and which ones a given network can evaluate, we would have both a fine-grained ruler and a to-do list of missing operations. Earlier results only described very low-degree cases, so a complete and practical characterization was needed.

## Contribution
**Necessary:** The paper (1) gives a full characterization of all equivariant graph polynomials via a concrete basis, where each basis element is a multi-graph and its evaluation is a tensor contraction; (2) provides algorithmic tools (tensor-contraction sequences plus a linear-time decision algorithm) to compute the exact polynomial expressive power of popular GNNs; and (3) uses the theory to enhance common architectures with polynomial features and new aggregations, yielding PPGN++.
**Additional:** This defines a new expressiveness hierarchy based on the degree of equivariant polynomials a model can compute, refining and connecting to the WL hierarchy.
**Audio script:** There are three contributions. First, a complete basis for all equivariant graph polynomials up to any degree, where each basis element corresponds to a directed multi-graph and computing it is a tensor contraction, an einsum. Second, algorithmic tools: the authors model a network as a bank of primitive contractions and give a linear-time algorithm that decides exactly which polynomials the network can compute, letting them tabulate the polynomial power of standard GNNs. Third, guided by this analysis they enhance existing models with precomputed polynomial features and extra operations, producing an improved provably powerful network they call PPGN++.

## Method
**Necessary:** Equivariant graph polynomials of degree at most d are characterized by a basis indexed by directed multi-graphs H with bounded vertices and edges; each basis element P_H is computed as a tensor contraction (einsum) over the input matrix, and is obtained by symmetrizing monomials with the Reynolds operator. A model is analyzed as a contraction bank F, and Algorithm 1 decides in linear time whether P_H is computable by node-based (F_n) or edge-based (F_e) banks. The theory then motivates PPGN++, a matrix-multiplication network augmented with additional primitive operations and precomputed polynomial features.
**Additional:** Node-based contractions recover 1-WL, edge-based contractions recover 2-FWL / 3-WL, and adding higher-degree polynomial features pushes expressive power strictly beyond 3-WL.
**Key equation:** `$Q_H(X) = \sum_{g \in S_n} g \cdot M_H\!\left(g^{-1}\cdot X,\ \delta_{g(a),g(b)}\right) = \sum_{g \in S_n} M_{g\cdot H}\!\left(X, \delta_{a,b}\right)$` ; PPGN++ layer: `$Z^{(k+1)} = \bar{m}_3\!\left[\ \bar{m}_1\!\left(Z^{(k)}, Z^{(k)\top}\right) \circledast \bar{m}_2\!\left(Z^{(k)}\right),\ Z^{(k)}\right]$`
**Audio script:** The core object is a basis for equivariant graph polynomials. Each basis element is a directed multi-graph H with a marked red edge, and its value on a graph is a tensor contraction, an einsum expression that multiplies entries of the adjacency matrix according to the edges of H and sums over the internal nodes. To make these polynomials equivariant, monomials are averaged over all node permutations with the Reynolds operator, which the paper shows equals a sum over the orbit of H. To analyze a network, the authors treat it as a bank of primitive contractions and give a linear-time algorithm that decides whether a target polynomial can be assembled by repeatedly contracting nodes. Node-based banks turn out to equal 1-WL and edge-based banks equal 2-FWL, also known as 3-WL. Building on this, PPGN++ refines the powerful matrix-multiplication network with a small set of primitive operations and, crucially, feeds in precomputed polynomial features, which lifts it strictly above 3-WL.

## Dataset / Benchmark
**Necessary:** Evaluation uses three molecular graph regression benchmarks, ZINC-12K, ZINC-full, and Alchemy, plus a Strongly Regular (SR) graph collection for testing the ability to distinguish hard non-isomorphic graphs.
**Additional:** ZINC-12K has a 10000/1000/1000 train/val/test split and ZINC-full has about 220011/24445/5000; all trained models respect a 500K parameter budget, following the protocols of Dwivedi et al. (2020) and Lim et al. (2022).
**Audio script:** The experiments cover two settings. For real-world performance, the authors use three standard molecular regression datasets: ZINC with twelve thousand molecules, the larger ZINC-full with over two hundred thousand, and Alchemy, all measured by test mean absolute error, lower being better, and all models kept under a five-hundred-thousand parameter budget. For raw expressive power they use families of Strongly Regular graphs, which are notoriously hard to tell apart and cannot be separated by 3-WL, measuring the failure rate at distinguishing non-isomorphic pairs.

## Key Result
**Necessary:** PPGN++ with high-degree polynomial features sets state of the art on all three regression benchmarks, reaching test MAE 0.071 on ZINC-12K, 0.020 on ZINC-full, and 0.109 on Alchemy, and precomputing polynomial features gives provably strictly better than 3-WL expressive power while remaining efficient.
**Additional:** Polynomial features also sharply improve weaker backbones: GatedGCN's ZINC-12K MAE drops from 0.265 to 0.106 once degree-6 polynomial features are added.
**Audio script:** The headline result is state of the art across the board. On ZINC-12K, PPGN++ with degree-six polynomial features reaches a test error of 0.071, beating strong baselines like CIN at 0.079 and GIN at 0.163. On ZINC-full it reaches 0.020, and on Alchemy 0.109, both best in class. Just as striking, the polynomial features are model-agnostic: bolting degree-six features onto a plain GatedGCN cuts its ZINC-12K error from 0.265 to 0.106. And because the features are precomputed, the network gains expressive power strictly beyond 3-WL without paying a heavy runtime cost.

First, degree matters: on ZINC-12K, adding degree-five features gives 0.072 and degree-six gives 0.071, a steady improvement over the base 0.076, and the same trend appears on the other datasets. Second, on the Strongly Regular graphs, which 3-WL cannot separate at all, adding polynomial features drives the failure rate down by several orders of magnitude as the degree climbs from five to seven, for both GatedGCN and PPGN++. ## Takeaway
**Necessary:** Grading and building graph networks by the equivariant polynomials they can compute, each a multi-graph evaluated as a tensor contraction, yields a finer expressiveness hierarchy than WL and, in practice, precomputed polynomial features push standard GNNs beyond 3-WL and to state-of-the-art accuracy on molecular benchmarks.
**Additional:** The basis plus the linear-time computability algorithm turn "how expressive is this GNN?" into a concrete, checkable question with an actionable answer.
**Audio script:** The lasting message is that expressive power becomes concrete and constructive when you phrase it as: which equivariant graph polynomials can this network compute? Every such polynomial is just a small multi-graph evaluated by a tensor contraction, giving a finer ruler than Weisfeiler-Lehman and a direct list of missing operations. Acting on that list, precomputed polynomial features push ordinary graph networks past the 3-WL barrier and to the best accuracy on molecular property prediction, all within a modest parameter budget.
